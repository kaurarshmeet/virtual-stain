### **Claude**  `2026-06-05 19:03`

It's a **downstream classification validation** script that uses HER2 scoring accuracy as a proxy to validate whether the BIM rankings are meaningful.

**What it does:**

1. **Trains** a ResNet18 (ImageNet-pretrained, fine-tuned for 15 epochs) on real BCI IHC training images. Labels (HER2 0/1+/2+/3+) are parsed directly from patch filenames (e.g. `patch_3+.png`).

2. **Evaluates** that frozen classifier on three test sets:
   - Real BCI IHC — serves as the upper bound
   - BCI-trained model's generated BCI images — in-distribution
   - MIST-trained model's generated BCI images — cross-dataset

3. **Prints** accuracy and weighted F1 alongside all the pre-computed metrics (FID, PSNR, SSIM, layer-wise PHV, NDC, MSIC, Spearman) that are hardcoded in the `evaluate()` calls.

**The core hypothesis** it's testing: if BIM is a valid evaluation signal, the ranking `Real > BCI-model > MIST-model` should hold in classification accuracy — i.e., images that score better on NDC/MSIC/Spearman should also better preserve the HER2-diagnostic content that a classifier relies on.

One thing worth noting: the pre-computed metric values are hardcoded as arguments (`fid=54.28`, `bim_ndc=0.662`, etc.) — they're just printed alongside the classification results for comparison, not computed live. So this script assumes `evaluate.py` has already been run on those result directories.

\section{Introduction}

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of pathological tissue is commonly performed to diagnose disease, stage and grade cancers, estimate prognosis, and monitor treatment; however, traditional staining is time-consuming, and the antibodies required for IHC staining can be prohibitively expensive \cite{lin2025virtual}. Virtual IHC staining from H\&E (hematoxylin-and-eosin)-stained tissue images increases efficiency and convenience and provides a potential solution. From a computer vision standpoint, virtual H\&E-to-IHC staining is an interesting problem because it poses its own challenges that differentiate it from natural image translation: the size of tissue slide data, homogenization of image features in histology, and the invisibility of certain features in histology images, to name a few. \textbf{The input to our algorithm is H\&E-stained tissue images. We use Adaptive Supervised PatchNCE and DAIR-SQ Adaptive Supervised PatchNCE (our own proposed modification) to output virtually-stained IHC-stained tissue images.}

In $2025$, \textit{Lin et al.} conducted a comprehensive review of challenges and limitations within the field of virtual staining for pathology. We focus on three limitations they highlight in all current virtual staining approaches; to our knowledge, our work is novel as it addresses these limitations of current state-of-the-art models.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Limited generalizability: Existing virtual staining models show a decline in performance when tested on samples from different datasets than they were trained on.
    \item Limited robustness: If two input H\&E images differ only in hue and saturation, diagnostic information like distribution of cells and tissues should be the same for both images; ideally, then, the virtual IHC generated from both images should be identical. However, existing models generate notably different IHC. This indicates the models are acquiring spurious knowledge relating to color variability, rather than relying on morphological features.
    \item Limited evaluation methods: Current evaluation methods (FID, KID, PSNR, SSIM) emphasize pixel-level accuracy. However, for pathological virtual staining, metrics should measure whether generated stained images convey the same diagnostic information as ground truth images, rather than pixel-level alignment. 
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
Virtual staining has emerged as an application of image-to-image translation in computational pathology, aiming to reduce the cost and time associated with laboratory staining procedures. Recent advances have adapted generative adversarial networks (GANs) and image translation methods such as pix2pix, CycleGAN, CUT, and diffusion-based models to this task. These methods have demonstrated the feasibility of generating visually realistic IHC images from H\&E inputs while preserving diagnostically relevant tissue structures.

The Adaptive Supervised PatchNCE (ASP) framework proposed by Li et al. is an influential contribution and addresses the challenge of imperfect alignment between H\&E and IHC image pairs. Because paired H\&E and IHC slides are obtained from adjacent tissue sections rather than the exact same slice, pixel-perfect correspondence is impossible. ASP introduces a contrastive learning objective that reduces the impact of noisy supervision and improves stain translation quality. The paper  also introduced the Multi-IHC Stain Translation (MIST) dataset, which contains paired H\&E and IHC images for several breast cancer biomarkers, including HER2, ER, PR, and Ki67.

Despite substantial progress, recent reviews have identified several limitations of existing virtual staining approaches as explained in the introduction.

Based on these observations, we went on to study H\&E-to-IHC virtual staining using paired pathology datasets. But instead of focusing solely on handling registration inconsistencies, we investigate whether training models to be invariant to color perturbations can improve robustness and cross-dataset generalization. We further evaluate model performance using biologically informed metrics(BIM)that measure preservation of clinically relevant features such as nuclear density, spatial cellular organization, and membrane staining intensity, complementing traditional image quality metrics.

\section{Data}
In our experiments, we use two publicly available paired H\&E-to-IHC virtual staining datasets introduced by \textit{Lin et al.}: the Breast Cancer Immunohistochemistry (BCI) dataset and the Multi-IHC Stain Translation (MIST) dataset. Both datasets contain paired H\&E and HER2 IHC image patches extracted from breast cancer whole-slide images and are commonly used benchmarks for virtual staining.

BCI dataset has 51 paired whole-slide image samples and around 4,870 aligned image patch pairs at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels. The MIST dataset has 64 paired HER2 whole-slide image samples and approximately 5,642 aligned image patch pairs of the same resolution. Both datasets were constructed using adjacent tissue sections stained separately with H\&E and HER2 IHC, resulting in aligned but not perfectly corresponding image pairs.

To evaluate model robustness to staining variability, we preprocessed by generating perturbed versions of both datasets. The perturbations modify the image hue and saturation while preserving underlying tissue morphology and spatial structure. 

Now with pertubation, our experiments utilize four datasets in total: the original BCI and MIST datasets, and the perturbed BCI and MIST datasets. This setup enables both in-distribution and out-of-distribution evaluation. By training and testing across original and perturbed domains, we can directly measure model robustness and cross-dataset generalization under realistic staining variations.

Before training, image patches were resized and normalized according to the preprocessing pipeline used by the underlying virtual staining framework. The perturbation process  preserved diagnostic tissue structures while introducing controlled color shifts, ensuring that any performance changes reflect sensitivity to staining appearance rather than changes in biological content.

\section{Methods}
We hypothesize that the generalizability problem and robustness problem can be tackled jointly. Consider the generalizability problem, where models fail to generalize to different datasets than the sources of their training sets: differences between pathology datasets can be largely attributed to differences in stain manufacturers, stain protocols, microscope/scanner hardware, and postprocessing techniques from different laboratories \cite{khan2025}. This observation parallels the robustness problem; when we artificially perturb the hue and saturation of H\&E images while preserving morphology, we are simulating effects that could be caused by different collection methods. The generalization problem and robustness problem, then, share the same underlying cause: reliance on non-morphological features. Consequently, addressing robustness through color-invariant training should simultaneously improve cross-dataset generalization.

\subsection{Adaptive Supervised PatchNCE}

Our model is built upon a CUT-based architecture, Adaptive Supervised PatchNCE \cite{li2023adaptive}. The objective is as follows: \begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_G = \lambda_{\text{GAN}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{GAN}} + \lambda_{\text{NCE}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}} + \lambda_{\text{GP}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{GP}} + \lambda_{\text{ASP}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ASP}}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{GAN}}$: Adversarial loss pushes the discriminator to classify generated IHC as real. 
\item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}$: Self-supervised NCE is a contrastive loss that ensures the generator preserves spatial tissue structure by pulling together features at matching spatial locations in the input (H\&E) and ouput (IHC). 
\item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{GP}}$: Gaussian Pyramid Reconstruction supervises pixel-level fidelity between real and generated IHC. (In this paper, we are more concerned about preserving diagnostic information, not pixel-to-pixel level accuracy.) 
\item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ASP}}$: Adaptive Supervised PatchNCE is a contrastive loss similar to NCE, except it pushes generated IHC closer to real IHC. 
\end{itemize}

We train for 30 epochs, since these are the suggested training options in the paper's github; we observed convergence of all models (plateau of loss) in this range of epochs and ran infence with the 25th epoch. \textbf{We built on the code in the ASP github directory, but the following contributions are our own: (1) Implementation of DAIR-SQ (see next section); (2) Analysis of hue, saturation, and value shift between BCI and MIST datasets to define perturbation functions; and (3) Definition of biologically informed metrics. Our code will address this more fully.}

\subsection{DAIR-SQ} 

Data Augmented Loss Invariant Regularization (DAIR) penalizes inconsistency in the generator's loss between original and augmented inputs \cite{huang2021robustness}. The intuition is as follows: Training the generator on an augmented dataset might allow the generator to learn or memorize color-specific mappings, whereas using a loss constrains the generator directly. Our augmentation function applies random HSV perturbations to the H\&E input: hue is shifted by a random angle sampled uniformly from $[-30°, 30°]$, saturation is scaled by a factor sampled from $[0.75, 1.25]$, and value is scaled by a factor from $[0.95, 1.05]$. These values reflect the inter-dataset color variation observed between BCI and MIST as examples of between-dataset variation. 

In each forward pass, $\mathcal{L}_G$ is computed twice: once on the original input $x$ and once on the perturbed input $\tilde{x}$. This gives $\mathcal{L}{\text{orig}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pert}}$ respectively. The DAIR-SQ regularizer is then:

$$\mathcal{R}{\text{sq}} = \left(\sqrt{\mathcal{L}{\text{orig}}} - \sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\text{pert}}}\right)^2$$

The total training objective is:

$$\mathcal{L}{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}{\text{orig}} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{R}_{\text{sq}}$$

\subsection{Biologically-Informed Metrics}

While pathological virtual staining is fundamentally an image-to-image translation task, there are requirements unique to pathology that stem from the overall goal to preserve diagnostic information \cite{klockner2025h}. In natural image translation, the goal is pixel-level accuracy or one-to-one correspondence, while in pathological virtual staining, the goal is preservation of diagnostic information \cite{lin2025virtual}. Currently, most virtual staining evaluations rely on natural image metrics. In the following section, we discuss the standard natural image metrics we will report and propose three new biologically informed metrics. 

\subsubsection{Standard Natural Image Metrics}
We report the following natural image metrics because they are commonly reported in the evaluation of the virtual staining task. 

\textbf{FID: }The Frechet Inception Distance (FID) measures the Wasserstein distance between embeddings of real and generated image distributions, assuming that both are Gaussian \cite{klockner2025h}.  Embeddings are generated using the Inception v3 model. The metric is defined as follows: 
\begin{equation}
\text{FID}(y, \hat{y}) = \|\mu_y - \mu_{\hat{y}}\|_2^2 + \text{Tr}\left(\Sigma_y + \Sigma_{\hat{y}} - 2(\Sigma_y \Sigma_{\hat{y}})^{1/2}\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $y$ and $\hat{y}$ represent real and generated image embeddings, respectively;  $\mu_y, \mu_{\hat{y}}$ represent mean feature vectors; and $\Sigma_y, \Sigma_{\hat{y}}$ are the covariance matrices. \\

\textbf{PSNR: } Peak signal-to-noise ratio measures the quality of reconstructed images and is defined as follows \cite{lin2025virtual}: 
\begin{equation}
\text{PSNR} = 10 \cdot \log_{10} \left( \frac{\text{MAX}_I^2}{\text{MSE}} \right)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{m^2} \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} \left[ I(i,j) - K(i,j) \right]^2
\end{equation}
Here, $\text{MAX}_I$ is the maximum possible pixel value of the image and $\text{MSE}$ is the mean squared error between the original and generated images (denoted as $I$ and $K$ respectively). PSNR is suboptimal in virtual staining scenarios, since ground-truth and source images lack good pixel-to-pixel correspondence \cite{klockner2025h}. \\

\textbf{SSIM:} Structural Similarity Index considers luminance ($l$), contrast ($c$), and structure ($s$) of images \cite{klockner2025h}. 
\begin{equation}
    \text{SSIM}(y, \hat{y}) = [l(y, \hat{y})]^{\alpha} \cdot [c(y, \hat{y})]^{\beta} \cdot [s(y, \hat{y})]^{\gamma}
    \label{eq:ssim}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    l(y, \hat{y}) = \frac{2\mu_y \mu_{\hat{y}} + C_1}{\mu_y^2 + \mu_{\hat{y}}^2 + C_1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    c(y, \hat{y}) = \frac{2\sigma_y \sigma_{\hat{y}} + C_2}{\sigma_y^2 + \sigma_{\hat{y}}^2 + C_2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    s(y, \hat{y}) = \frac{\sigma_{y\hat{y}} + C_3}{\sigma_y \sigma_{\hat{y}} + C_3}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mu_y$ and $\mu_{\hat{y}}$ are the mean intensities; $\sigma_y$ and $\sigma_{\hat{y}}$ are the standard deviations; $\sigma_{y\hat{y}}$ is the covariance, $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ are component weights; and $C_1$, $C_2$, $C_3$ are constants added to avoid instability. SSIM is more robust than PSNR to small distortions and thus better-suited for the task than PSNR.

\subsubsection{Biologically-Informed Metrics}
HER2 scoring focuses on two relevant diagnostic features of IHC images: (1) quantity of infiltrating carcinoma cells; (2) intensity of membrane staining \cite{peng2024advancing}. \textit{Peng et al.} extract this information from training data and integrate it into GAN training but measure performance using natural image metrics. We adapt their approach to propose three biologically informed metrics: nuclear density consistency to address the first diagnostic criteria and membrane staining intensity consistency for the second. The merit of having two distinct metrics is we can see which biological signal is lacking in GAN output.

\textbf{Nuclear Density Consistency}
\textit{Peng et al.} calculate the difference in nuclei counts between real and generated IHC images and create an error-distribution histogram over all test images. We formalize this idea to define nuclear density consistency (NDC), a scalar metric, as follows. First, each IHC image is decomposed into  hematotoxylin (H) and diaminobenzidine (DAB) channels via Ruifrok and Johnston deconvolution \cite{skimage_color, ruifrok2001quantification}.  Then, Cellpose's 'nuclei' model is applied to channel H, and the 'cyto2' model to channel DAB to obtain two nuclei segmentation masks\cite{stringer2021cellpose}.  The total nuclei count is obtained by combining nuclei counts from both masks. Here, nuclei counts in the real and generated images are $n_{y}$ and $n_{\hat{y}}$ respectively and $\epsilon$ is a small numerical constant. Then NDC for one image is: 
$$\text{NDC} = \text{clip}!\left(1 - \frac{|n_{\hat{y}} - n_y|}{n_y + \epsilon},\ 0,\ 1\right)$$
When  $n_{y} =0, n_{\hat{y}} > n_y$ (model hallucinated nuclei) or when $n_{\hat{y}} = 0$ and $n_{y} > n_{\hat{y}}$ (model failed to catch any nuclei), it receives a score of $0$. When $n_{\hat{y}} = n_{y} = 0$, the model receives a score of 1. NDC over the entire dataset is the mean of NDC for each image. Importantly, since NDC is defined as a ratio, dataset-level differences in average nuclei count per patch don't effect evaluation; NDC is dataset-invariant, unlike the absolute count differences used by \textit{Peng et al. }

\textbf{Spearman Density Consistency}
NDC quantifies the difference in nuclei counts between real and generated images but fails to consider spatial arrangement. We propose Spearman Density Consistency (SDC) to supplement NDC. We combine nuclei segmentation masks for the H and DAB channels into one mask; then, we construct a nuclei probability distribution map by placing a 1 at each nucleus location and applying Gaussian blurring. Then, we compute the Spearman correlation between maps made from real and generated images to see if spatial arrangement is preserved. 

\textbf{Membrane Staining Intensity Consistency}
\textit{Peng et al.} evaluate membrane staining intensity as follows: The DAB channel (see NDC section) is rendered as RGB and converted to HSV. Then, by applying saturation thresholds $t \in [0.1, 0.5]$ with step size $0.02$ to the DAB saturation channel, they obtain the ratio of pixels that meet the threshold in real/generated images and compute the discrepancy over all test images. We formalize this as membrane staining intensity consistency (MISC) as follows: For each threshold $t \in [0.1, 0.5]$ (step size $0.02$), we compute the ratio of pixels exceeding $t$, $r_{y}(t)$ and $r_{\hat{y}}(t)$, for real and generated images respectively. MISC for a single image is: 
$$\text{MSIC} = 1 - \frac{\frac{1}{T}\sum_t |r_{\hat{y}}(t) - r_y(t)|}{\frac{1}{T}\sum_t r_y(t) + \frac{1}{T}\sum_t r_{\hat{y}}(t) + \epsilon}$$
Here, $T$ is the number of thresholds applied and $\epsilon$ is a small numerical constant for consistency. MISC over the entire dataset is the mean of MISC for each image.

\section{Conclusion + Future Work}
Our original hypothesis that both robustness and generalizability could be tackled together was mistaken; we see that DAIR-SQ implementation aided consistently in the robustness task, but did not provide consistent results for the generalizability task. This may be due to inter-dataset differences between MIST and BCI beyond what we captured (hue, saturation, and value variance). Furthermore, our results showed that robustness degradations was smaller than anticipated across all models. Qualitative analysis revealed that failures occur when generated IHC outputs have no distinguishable H or DAB channels to be deconvoluted, leading to poor performance metrics. For future work, we plan to extend the evaluation to full whole-slide images and propose additional biologically informed measures such as internuclear distance, cross-sectional nuclear area, eccentricity, and cell compactness to see how these more fine-grained features cause improvements.
